WEDNESDAY. MARCH 14, 1926

"BRAVE MEN AND GOOD FIREMEN."

To describe yesterday evening's tragedy as a profound shock to Kalamazoo is to express all too mildly the reaction which the people of this city feel toward the loss of two of their bravest and manliest fellow citizens. We do not wish to indulge in tritely phrased eulogies nor sing a requiem of flowery words, yet we do feel that the highest tribute is not too much to pay to men who have themselves paid their lives as the price of devotion to duty. As Chief Russell put it, we read of these things happening every day in other cities, but when a tragedy of this kind strikes home it is a different matter. And so it is.

The fire department annals of many American communities contain accounts of courageous deeds and heroic sacrifices made by the men who are "always ready," but it takes something closer to home to make us fully appreciate the true significance of the fireman's code of honor.

The destruction of the First Methodist Church edifice, a famous landmark and venerable house of worship though it was, is all but obscured by the inestimably greater loss which its burning entailed. We are certain that the officers and members of the church will be among the first to recognize this. For there is something in the story of John Morren and Claus Slater which gives us new assurance that the world still has men who do not hesitate to hazard their lives in pursuance of their duties, that loyalty to the highest human principles may be sealed as bravely in peace as in war, and that the so-called "softening" influences of modern civilization have not deprived society of men who can face danger as men should.

Heroes? We could go on forever if we wished to argue the question of what constitutes a hero. But we don't think it is necessary to quibble over that. When men are literally struck down in the line of duty, there is no reason whatever to be the least bit niggardly with the praise we ascribe to them.

"Brave men and good firemen" was Chief Russell's brief but thoroughly sufficient comment; we will let it go at that and call it the finest tribute which any commander can pay to those who have served under him. Surely John Morren and Claus Slater would regard it as such. So would the five others who faced death with them in the treacherous recesses of the burning structure.

Kalamazoo is justly proud of John Morren and Claus Slater, but we are certain that even this great feeling of pride is far overshadowed by deep and genuine sympathy for the wives and children who bear the real brunt of yesterday's tragic happening. No words of comfort or consolation can soften more than slightly the anguish of their trial; no eulogies of the dead can help them bear a loss so bitter. We only hope that their spirits may be strengthened by the knowledge that the men they mourn died as men. And it is not to be thought, for one moment, that the people of Kalamazoo will allow the bereaved ones to endure any kind of suffering which it is within the people's power to prevent.
