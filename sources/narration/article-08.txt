RECOVERED IN TWO HOURS
It was nearly two hours before the bodies of the dead firemen were finally dug from the ruins. The rescuers worked in shifts.

Two Kalamazoo firemen were killed, and five others were injured Saturday evening in a fire which swept the First Methodist Church at Rose and Lovell Streets to complete ruin. The loss is estimated at $100,000.

The firemen met death when they were caught beneath a falling balcony at the north end of the church. The balcony fell in as the south side of the big brick tower gave way. Tons of brick and heavy beams came down upon the heads of the firemen almost without warning. Those who escaped barely managed to reach the front vestibule door. Others were caught underneath the wreckage and were helpless until rescued by fellow firemen.

As the three men were holding the line of hose, others, who had moved about six feet to one side, heard a crash above and noticed the roof of the second balcony—about 40 feet in the air—give way. They called to their companions to "run for your lives" and dashed to the door. Leyden, thought to have been the nearest to the door, just crossed the threshold as the falling brick and timbers crashed to the floor. Kruizenga and one or two other firemen also reached safety, but not before falling brick had struck their feet.

Slater and Morren did not reach the door. Both men had gotten within a foot of safety when their bodies were carried through the floor to a depth of two feet. They both fell within a few inches of each other and were undoubtedly crushed to death.

FORMS RESCUE SQUAD
The first inkling firemen on the outside of the building had of disaster was when Leyden and others rushed out, shouting that their two companions had been buried beneath the falling roof. Chief Russell immediately organized a rescue party, and a dozen firemen and policemen, armed with axes, shovels, and lanterns, invaded the building, imperiling their own lives as portions of the roof and sections of the brick walls were still sliding to the main floor. No sign of the two firemen could be found at first.

Firemen, policemen, and officers from the sheriff's department began digging, throwing out the timbers and debris as fast as they could. They were severely handicapped for a moment by the high-powered streams of water that were still being played on the ruins and also by darkness. A big searchlight was wired up by Sheriff Borden and suspended over the workmen.

FIND FIRST BODY
The rescuers dug to a depth of one foot before any trace of either fireman was found. Then one shovel struck the leather-coated body of Slater. "I've found one body," informed a fireman. Then they started work with their hands, heaving away the bricks and other debris until half the buried fireman's body was uncovered. Slater was lying face downward. The firemen thought it would be but a few moments before the body would be unearthed, but it required exactly one hour and ten minutes to extricate the body of the first victim.

A huge timber had lodged across Slater's limbs, and firemen had to cut this away with axes. Care had to be used to avoid striking the body, and the firemen who were doing the work were in constant danger of plunging through the floor to the basement. It was necessary for three firemen to hold the body of Slater to keep it from sinking head-downward into the basement.

FELL FACE DOWNWARD
Finally, the body was freed and was carried to the H.S. Harrington morgue in the police ambulance. At that time, the body of Morren had not been found. Digging to the eastward of the place where Slater's body had been found, firemen located Morren's body, buried deep in the debris. He, too, had fallen face downward, having been struck just as he was about to pass through the doorway. Less difficulty was experienced in extricating Morren's body, and this gruesome task was completed in half an hour. His body was taken to the Harrington morgue.

CHIEF RUSSELL WEEPS
Tears coursed down the fire-blackened cheeks of Chief Russell, who had battled the flames for more than an hour and who had warned all his men to be careful in entering the building when the bodies of Slater and Morren were extricated from the ruins.

"Brave men and good firemen," was his exclamation in a broken voice. "We read of these things happening in other cities every day, but when a tragedy of this kind strikes home, it is a different matter." He stated that the men who entered the shell of the building took their lives in their hands, but added further that he had not ordered them so far into the building. If they had fought the burning ruins from the doorway of the first lobby, they would not have been killed, he stated.

The bodies of the firemen were lying across the hose which they had been operating.

MEMBER OF FIREMEN'S BAND
Slater had been in the fire department about a year, the chief stated, and was the driver of the hook and ladder truck at Central Station. He was also prominent and popular in the firemen's band and was well-liked by his companions. He leaves a wife and three children.

Morren was stationed at Number 5 Station in Douglas Avenue and had been in the department two years. He was married last June. He leaves two children by a former wife, who died.

James Raven was injured when he entered the basement at the rear to locate the blaze. It is believed he fell, severely injuring one knee. He was carried from the burning building by companions and taken to Old Borgess Hospital in the police car.

TWO NEARLY SUFFOCATED
The other injured firemen sustained their cuts and bruises while fighting the fire from within. Two of the men were nearly suffocated.

The church building was empty of firemen when the roof caved in.

When firemen reached the scene, the church was virtually obscured by smoke, which spread in clouds from every window and crevice. They were handicapped by the density of smoke, and those men entering the basement and other portions of the building were required to wear gas masks.

The fire was located in the basement at the rear of the church as soon as firemen arrived. At that time, it had reached nearly to the roof at the back end, before hose could be played on all parts of the building. The roof had blazed forth. It was seen then by Chief Russell that the building would go in spite of any efforts his men could make. He ordered the firemen not to enter the building.

ICE SAVES TWO HOUSES
A dozen streams of hose forced water on virtually every portion of the building, some wetting down the side walls, others crashing through the windows to the interior, and two playing on the home of Frank Russell, directly east of the church, and that of A.H. Rothermel, directly south of the structure. Fear was expressed for the safety of both these homes as the flames and firebrands were shooting in the direction of both. The Rothermel home is located about 10 feet from the rear of the church, while the Russell home is about a rod and a half away. Ice forming on the roofs of these two homes probably saved them from catching fire.

The church burned with great rapidity. Just one hour after the fire was discovered, the building was in total ruins. The roof had caved in, and nothing stood but the bare brick walls. Wooden cornices at the tops of the walls burned, and a fiery border circled the top. Luckily, none of the four walls collapsed, but policemen roped off fire limits to protect the thousands of people who thronged about the corner to watch the fire.
