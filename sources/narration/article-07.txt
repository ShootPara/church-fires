WEDNESDAY, JULY 7, 1926

PRESBYTERIAN CHURCH FIRE: LOSS $100,000
Two Firemen Overcome by Smoke After Collapse of Roof.

DISCOVERED AT 11:20
The Presbyterian fire was discovered about 11:20 when the entire interior of the auditorium burst forth in flames. Some of the flames shot out the windows with the first puff.

While apparatus from Central Station a block away was responding to the alarm, patrolmen at the scene broke open the front doors so that firemen might carry hose lines into the structure with the least possible delay. Smoke filled every room in the structure, and the crackling of the flames could be heard from the street. Fire limits were immediately established by the police, and the work of fighting another big church fire got underway within five minutes after the alarm had sounded.

BURSTS INTO FLAME
The fire, which was the most spectacular of the church conflagrations to watch, is said to have started in or near the pipe organ at the rear of the pulpit. Patrolman Clare Stevens, who turned in the alarm, was the first person to discover the fire. He stated that suddenly the entire interior of the auditorium seemed to burst forth in flames, where a second before all had been darkness. Sergeant John Sperry of the police department saw the first spurt of flames two blocks away.

Upon arrival at the church, Chief Charles H. Russell and several men from Central Station started inside the building through the front entrance. They saw the flames as they shot toward the ceiling in the rear of the auditorium and they sought to fight them at close range. Just as they entered, the main auditorium plaster began falling and the smoke began to thicken. So fierce became the roaring furnace, and great the possibility of injury or death from the falling ceiling, that Chief Russell and his men retreated from the building.

It was while inside the church at the very beginning of the fire that the two firemen, A.C. VanHaaften and Gaare DeRight, were almost overcome by smoke. They were helped from the building by other firemen, and after a rest of a few moments, were unable to resume their posts.

ROOF CRASHES
The front portion of the roof burned rapidly, and just a few moments before it fell into the mass of ruins, the rear portion of the roof burst into flame. The roof of the First Presbyterian Church seemed to burn more rapidly than the roofs of other churches. The firemen were unable to quell the flames on the roof, despite the fact that six different lines of hose were playing water on the top of the building. As the two sections of the church roof fell, a great, resounding puff, not unlike a stifled explosion, was heard, and the flames from the interior of the building shot high into the air, while huge firebrands hurtled from the inside ruins over the traced shell of the walls. Startled by the crash, the crowds started a sudden rush away from the building, fearing that the walls might fall.

After the roof had fallen, the firemen gradually subdued the flames on the inside. Seeing it would be impossible to save the structure itself, Chief Russell concentrated all his efforts on saving the adjoining buildings from igniting. A line of hose was strung to the top floor of the Pythian Building, located just ten feet east of the Presbyterian Church, while another line was carried to the roof of the Y.W.C.A. Lines of water, manned from the ground, were used intermittently to wet the sides of the adjoining buildings which faced the fire.

The walls of the Y.W.C.A., north of the church in South Rose Street, became so heated that a person could not bear his hand against them. The windows in the west side of the Pythian Building were either broken or cracked by the heat. Eight of ten men who were in the Pythian Building, under the leadership of Ralph Chapman, climbed to the fire escape on the west side of the building and wet down the east roof of the church building with fire extinguishers and hose lines from the interior of the Pythian block. When the heat became too intense, the men returned inside the building and poured chemicals from the windows.

Fear was freely expressed that the Pythian Building, which has been on fire two different times within the last eight months, might be ignited by the flames from the church fire, and every precaution was taken to warn everyone in the building of the danger. Officers made a tour of every floor of the building as soon as the church fire started, and people who have offices in the building were notified that they might take from their offices as much of value as they could, pending a possible outbreak in the Pythian building.

Guests, officials, and employees at the Y.W.C.A. Building were routed from their sleep by firemen and officers, and all the occupants were ordered downstairs to the main lobby. The Y.W.C.A., however, was in less danger of ignition than the Pythian Building due to the fact that the light breeze carried the flames in the opposite direction.

The lives of Chief Russell and six of his firemen were endangered when a large section of the west portion of the roof loosened and came crashing to the street on the south side of the building. The chief and his men were engaged at the time, and were in a group moving two or three hose lines about 15 feet from the south wall. As the section of the roof started to slide, the crowd of spectators cried out, and the firemen, looking up, saw the oncoming shower of bricks and timbers. They made a wild dash for safety and reached the center of the street just as the debris struck the ground. Loosened slates from the roof and bricks and heavy pieces of charred wood were raining down continuously during the fire, endangering the firemen and making it difficult to move the hose lines close to the building.

After the fire had abated to some extent, the front entrance of the church was opened, and two lines of hose forced water into the southwest spire, saving it from being gutted. The same move was made in the northwest spire, and that section was also saved from complete ruin.

The entire downtown section of the city and residence areas for many blocks away were clouded with smoke from the fire, and during the time the flames were at their height, the reflection could be seen many miles away from the city. Although there was not a heavy breeze Tuesday night, firebrands were thrown high into the air and were carried as far as a block away.

The fire started at 11:20, and at 2:45 the smoke and fire had cleared away to expose jagged walls of smoke-blackened brick, with charred timbers, twisted iron rods, and debris leaning in a haphazard manner. Inside was a mass of twisted, smoking ruins which about three hours before had been a part of the city's largest church building. The interior of the auditorium was a complete loss, and virtually every other portion of the building was damaged beyond remodeling. The southeastern section fared the best during the fire, and although the roof was burned and fell inside, the interior did not burst forth into flames.

Seeing it would be impossible to save the interior of the church, following the first attempt of firemen to enter the building, Chief Russell issued strict orders that no one was to enter the burning building. When a detail of firemen started with a hose line toward the front entrance, Chief Russell ordered them away. He would not permit his men to go through the doors of the gutted structure until dawn, and he himself surveyed the smoldering ruins from the top floor and roof of the Pythian Block, just 10 feet away. The chief took every precaution to protect the lives of his men, and for three hours after the flames had died down, the firemen remained on the outside to play the streams of water.
