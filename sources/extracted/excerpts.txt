Congregational Church Fire

TUESDAY, DECEMBER 29, 1925

Arthur Stoops, 312 John Street, and John Triestram, 906 Boerman Avenue, two boys, were passing the church on their way to the Y.M.C.A. when their attention was drawn by the cracking of glass in one of the windows on the main floor. Triestram dashed to the Y.M.C.A. and telephoned the fire department.

By the time the first fire companies reached the scene, heavy black smoke was rolling in great volumes from the basement windows on the east side of the building. As fast as the firemen arrived, more lines of three-inch hose were put into action, and the men concentrated their efforts in pouring great streams of water into the basement, which they believed was the seat of the fire.

The fire was discovered at 12:25. At 1:05 p.m., the entire roof of the west wing of the church collapsed. Instantly, the entire building was a roaring furnace within. Ten lines of hose were then pouring streams of water into the flames. Firemen had climbed to the fire escapes and roof of the Y.M.C.A. to the north in order to battle with the flames at a better advantage.

It was more than an hour, however, before they had the fire under control, and buildings immediately surrounding were saved. The First Reformed Church is adjacent to the east and the Y.M.C.A. building, and the First Baptist Church stand a short distance away to the north.

The solid brick walls of the church withstood the fierceness of the roaring flames from within and kept the fire from spreading to the nearby building, in spite of the fact that there was a slight breeze from the northwest.

Every available piece of fire apparatus in the city was summoned after the call had been received at Central fire station. Many firemen who were off duty for the day were called back into service.

Several pipemen, among the first to reach the fire, managed to work their way through the dense smoke into the basement of the building, but it was so suffocating they were unable to remain inside for more than a few moments. While they were pouring water into the basement windows to the east, someone suddenly discovered a tongue of flame leaping from the peak of the roof.

More hoses were called into play, and firemen climbed to points of vantage in an effort to head off the flames. By this time, however, the main auditorium of the church was in flames, and the holes which broke through the roof opened the disastrous draft which the firemen had hoped to prevent.

Hundreds of people who were on the streets during the noon hour were attracted to the scene by the great volumes of yellow-black smoke which swept low into the streets in the business section, darkening the entire city. Fire lines had to be strung and the crowds were held back in safety, as it was feared the big walls might collapse at any moment. Firebrands dropped like snowflakes all over the business district.

The big brick tower of the church remained standing throughout the fire, although it made the work of the firemen extremely hazardous, as it was feared it might topple over at any moment.

The flames, it is believed, swept through the entire building following the furnace pipes and ventilating system. The tons of water that were poured into the flames were believed to be sufficient to extinguish the blaze.

Fire engine companies No. 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, and 6 responded with full apparatus, and No. 3 company from the East Side was drawn into Central station to stand ready for other emergencies.

Methodist Church Fire

SUNDAY, MARCH 14, 1926

The fire was discovered at 6:00 p.m. and, although the church is within a block of Central Fire Station, the entire roof was enveloped in flames before the firemen could reach the scene. Every available piece of fire apparatus in the city was thrown into action. All firemen off duty were called into service, and Kalamazoo's entire force of firemen were swiftly brought into service.

The fire is believed to have started in the south end of the basement of the church and was due either to an overheated furnace or to defective wiring, in the opinion of Fire Chief Charles H. Russell.

For nearly an hour, the firemen battled with the flames from all sides, the blaze being too fierce for the men to enter the building. Thousands of tons of water were thrown into the flames, but the fire had gained such headway during the first few minutes that it was soon out of control. Frame buildings to the south and east of the church were saved from destruction by being drenched with a continuous downpour of water.

The fire was virtually under control when the tragedy which snuffed out the lives of two of Kalamazoo's most popular firemen occurred.

They had been sent in as a relief squad to firemen who had battled with the blaze from the start. Grasping a line of hose, Slater and Morren, in company with Firemen Ben Kruizenga, Martin Leyden, and others, entered the main entrance to the church to play water on the burning ruins inside.

They had reached the second doors, which open directly into the church proper, and had passed through the west doorway to a distance of about five feet.

As the three men were holding the line of hose, others, who had moved about six feet to one side, heard a crash above and noticed the roof of the second balcony—about 40 feet in the air—give way. They called to their companions to "run for your lives," and dashed to the door. Leyden, thought to have been the nearest to the door, had just crossed the threshold as the falling brick and timbers crashed to the floor. Kruizenga and one or two other firemen also reached safety, but not before falling brick had struck their feet.

A dozen streams of hose forced water on virtually every portion of the building, some wetting down the side walls, others crashing through the windows to the interior, and two playing on the home of Frank Russell, directly east of the church, and that of A.H. Rothermel, directly south of the structure. Fear was expressed for the safety of both these homes as the flames and firebrands were shooting in the direction of both. The Rothermel home is located about 10 feet from the rear of the church, while the Russell home is about a rod and a half away. Ice forming on the roofs of these two homes probably saved them from catching fire.

The church burned with great rapidity. Just one hour after the fire was discovered, the building was in total ruins. The roof had caved in, and nothing stood but the bare brick walls. Wooden cornices at the tops of the walls burned, and a fiery border circled the top. Luckily, none of the four walls collapsed, but policemen roped off fire limits to protect the thousands of people who thronged about the corner to watch the fire.

Dense smoke clouded the entire neighborhood. Firemen at Central Station, just one block away, saw the smoke as it issued from the top of the structure and thus received the alarm. Just as the department was responding, an alarm came in from D.S. Field.

Every piece of fire apparatus in the city was pressed into service, and pumpers and trucks were stationed at every corner within a radius of one block from the church. Six pumpers forced a maximum of 6,000 gallons of water a minute on the fire.

Seven thousand feet of hose were put into use, more than a dozen lines being extended from the pumpers. Every fireman in the city, including all reserves, were called into service and fought the flames for more than three hours. Water was poured onto the burning ruins for two hours following the crashing of the roof. 

Three different lines of hose burst during the fire, which further handicapped the firemen. These breakages were caused, Chief Russell stated, from excess pressure of the pumps.

Street car traffic was at a standstill during the fire, as power on the trolley wires had to be cut to make it possible for firemen to direct streams of water on the flames from the Rose Street side.

As night fell and the temperature lowered, the firemen became ice-coated and soaked from the spray of water. The streets around the church were flooded to a depth of one to three inches with water.

The walls of the church stood like ghostly spectres with a white coating of ice, and trees near the structure sparkled with frozen spray from the hose lines.

Frank Russell, who conducts a boarding house just east of the church, immediately opened his home to the firemen and prepared coffee and food for them. Other citizens sent sandwiches, and a number of city employees went to Central Station and prepared coffee, which was carried to the firefighters.

Presbyterian Church Fire

WEDNESDAY, JULY 7, 1926

The Presbyterian fire was discovered about 11:20 when the entire interior of the auditorium burst forth in flames. Some of the flames shot out the windows with the first puff.

While apparatus from Central Station a block away was responding to the alarm, patrolmen at the scene broke open the front doors so that firemen might carry hose lines into the structure with the least possible delay. Smoke filled every room in the structure, and the crackling of the flames could be heard from the street. Fire limits were immediately established by the police, and the work of fighting another big church fire got underway within five minutes after the alarm had sounded.

The fire, which was the most spectacular of the church conflagrations to watch, is said to have started in or near the pipe organ at the rear of the pulpit. Patrolman Clare Stevens, who turned in the alarm, was the first person to discover the fire. He stated that suddenly the entire interior of the auditorium seemed to burst forth in flames, where a second before all had been darkness. Sergeant John Sperry of the police department saw the first spurt of flames two blocks away.

Upon arrival at the church, Chief Charles H. Russell and several men from Central Station started inside the building through the front entrance. They saw the flames as they shot toward the ceiling in the rear of the auditorium and they sought to fight them at close range. Just as they entered, the main auditorium plaster began falling and the smoke began to thicken. So fierce became the roaring furnace, and great the possibility of injury or death from the falling ceiling, that Chief Russell and his men retreated from the building.

It was while inside the church at the very beginning of the fire that the two firemen, A.C. VanHaaften and Gaare DeRight, were almost overcome by smoke. They were helped from the building by other firemen, and after a rest of a few moments, were unable to resume their posts.

The front portion of the roof burned rapidly, and just a few moments before it fell into the mass of ruins, the rear portion of the roof burst into flame. The roof of the First Presbyterian Church seemed to burn more rapidly than the roofs of other churches. The firemen were unable to quell the flames on the roof, despite the fact that six different lines of hose were playing water on the top of the building. As the two sections of the church roof fell, a great, resounding puff, not unlike a stifled explosion, was heard, and the flames from the interior of the building shot high into the air, while huge firebrands hurtled from the inside ruins over the traced shell of the walls. Startled by the crash, the crowds started a sudden rush away from the building, fearing that the walls might fall.

After the roof had fallen, the firemen gradually subdued the flames on the inside. Seeing it would be impossible to save the structure itself, Chief Russell concentrated all his efforts on saving the adjoining buildings from igniting. A line of hose was strung to the top floor of the Pythian Building, located just ten feet east of the Presbyterian Church, while another line was carried to the roof of the Y.W.C.A. Lines of water, manned from the ground, were used intermittently to wet the sides of the adjoining buildings which faced the fire.

The walls of the Y.W.C.A., north of the church in South Rose Street, became so heated that a person could not bear his hand against them. The windows in the west side of the Pythian Building were either broken or cracked by the heat. Eight of ten men who were in the Pythian Building, under the leadership of Ralph Chapman, climbed to the fire escape on the west side of the building and wet down the east roof of the church building with fire extinguishers and hose lines from the interior of the Pythian block. When the heat became too intense, the men returned inside the building and poured chemicals from the windows.

Fear was freely expressed that the Pythian Building, which has been on fire two different times within the last eight months, might be ignited by the flames from the church fire, and every precaution was taken to warn everyone in the building of the danger. Officers made a tour of every floor of the building as soon as the church fire started, and people who have offices in the building were notified that they might take from their offices as much of value as they could, pending a possible outbreak in the Pythian building.

Guests, officials, and employees at the Y.W.C.A. Building were routed from their sleep by firemen and officers, and all the occupants were ordered downstairs to the main lobby. The Y.W.C.A., however, was in less danger of ignition than the Pythian Building due to the fact that the light breeze carried the flames in the opposite direction.

The lives of Chief Russell and six of his firemen were endangered when a large section of the west portion of the roof loosened and came crashing to the street on the south side of the building. The chief and his men were engaged at the time, and were in a group moving two or three hose lines about 15 feet from the south wall. As the section of the roof started to slide, the crowd of spectators cried out, and the firemen, looking up, saw the oncoming shower of bricks and timbers. They made a wild dash for safety and reached the center of the street just as the debris struck the ground. Loosened slates from the roof and bricks and heavy pieces of charred wood were raining down continuously during the fire, endangering the firemen and making it difficult to move the hose lines close to the building.

After the fire had abated to some extent, the front entrance of the church was opened, and two lines of hose forced water into the southwest spire, saving it from being gutted. The same move was made in the northwest spire, and that section was also saved from complete ruin.

The entire downtown section of the city and residence areas for many blocks away were clouded with smoke from the fire, and during the time the flames were at their height, the reflection could be seen many miles away from the city. Although there was not a heavy breeze Tuesday night, firebrands were thrown high into the air and were carried as far as a block away.

The fire started at 11:20, and at 2:45 the smoke and fire had cleared away to expose jagged walls of smoke-blackened brick, with charred timbers, twisted iron rods, and debris leaning in a haphazard manner. Inside was a mass of twisted, smoking ruins which about three hours before had been a part of the city's largest church building. The interior of the auditorium was a complete loss, and virtually every other portion of the building was damaged beyond remodeling. The southeastern section fared the best during the fire, and although the roof was burned and fell inside, the interior did not burst forth into flames.

Seeing it would be impossible to save the interior of the church, following the first attempt of firemen to enter the building, Chief Russell issued strict orders that no one was to enter the burning building. When a detail of firemen started with a hose line toward the front entrance, Chief Russell ordered them away. He would not permit his men to go through the doors of the gutted structure until dawn, and he himself surveyed the smoldering ruins from the top floor and roof of the Pythian Block, just 10 feet away. The chief took every precaution to protect the lives of his men, and for three hours after the flames had died down, the firemen remained on the outside to play the streams of water.
