TUESDAY, DECEMBER 29, 1925

THE KALAMAZOO GAZETTECONGREGATIONAL CHURCH BURNS

$175,000 STRUCTURE DESTROYED AT NOONStarts Near Heating Plant; Boys Give Alarm; Adjacent Edifices Saved; Smoke Darkens City.

The First Congregational Church; Park and Academy streets, one of Kalamazoo's oldest and most beautiful churches, was completely destroyed by fire Tuesday noon, in one of the worst conflagrations that has visited this city in many years. The building was valued at $175,000. The loss was partly covered by $100,000 insurance.

The fire is believed to have started in the basement of the church, near the heating plant. C. E. Price, West Cedar street, the janitor, left the building at noon. Thirty minutes later the interior of the building was in flames.

BOYS DISCOVER FIRE:Arthur Stoops, 312 John Street, and John Triestram, 906 Boerman Avenue, two boys, were passing the church on their way to the Y.M.C.A. when their attention was drawn by the cracking of glass in one of the windows on the main floor. Triestram dashed to the Y.M.C.A. and telephoned the fire department.

By the time the first fire companies reached the scene, heavy black smoke was rolling in great volumes from the basement windows on the east side of the building. As fast as the firemen arrived, more lines of three-inch hose were put into action, and the men concentrated their efforts in pouring great streams of water into the basement, which they believed was the seat of the fire.

INTERIOR ROARING FURNACE:The fire was discovered at 12:25. At 1:05 p.m., the entire roof of the west wing of the church collapsed. Instantly, the entire building was a roaring furnace within. Ten lines of hose were then pouring streams of water into the flames. Firemen had climbed to the fire escapes and roof of the Y.M.C.A. to the north in order to battle with the flames at a better advantage.

It was more than an hour, however, before they had the fire under control, and buildings immediately surrounding were saved. The First Reformed Church is adjacent to the east and the Y.M.C.A. building, and the First Baptist Church stand a short distance away to the north.

SAVE NEARBY BUILDINGSThe solid brick walls of the church withstood the fierceness of the roaring flames from within and kept the fire from spreading to the nearby building, in spite of the fact that there was a slight breeze from the northwest.

Every available piece of fire apparatus in the city was summoned after the call had been received at Central fire station. Many firemen who were off duty for the day were called back into service.

FLAME LEAPS FROM ROOFSeveral pipemen, among the first to reach the fire, managed to work their way through the dense smoke into the basement of the building, but it was so suffocating they were unable to remain inside for more than a few moments. While they were pouring water into the basement windows to the east, someone suddenly discovered a tongue of flame leaping from the peak of the roof.

More hoses were called into play, and firemen climbed to points of vantage in an effort to head off the flames. By this time, however, the main auditorium of the church was in flames, and the holes which broke through the roof opened the disastrous draft which the firemen had hoped to prevent.

ESTABLISH FIRE LINESHundreds of people who were on the streets during the noon hour were attracted to the scene by the great volumes of yellow-black smoke which swept low into the streets in the business section, darkening the entire city. Fire lines had to be strung and the crowds were held back in safety, as it was feared the big walls might collapse at any moment. Firebrands dropped like snowflakes all over the business district.

The big brick tower of the church remained standing throughout the fire, although it made the work of the firemen extremely hazardous, as it was feared it might topple over at any moment.

TONS OF WATER USEDThe flames, it is believed, swept through the entire building following the furnace pipes and ventilating system. The tons of water that were poured into the flames were believed to be sufficient to extinguish the blaze.

Fire engine companies No. 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, and 6 responded with full apparatus, and No. 3 company from the East Side was drawn into Central station to stand ready for other emergencies.

BUILT IN 1890The Congregational Church, which is the largest Protestant church structure in Kalamazoo, was erected in 1890, replacing the first church originally built on the same site nearly 50 years before. The contract for the new church was awarded to Glanfield and Son, builders, who came to Kalamazoo and secured, in rapid succession, contracts for erection of the original Y.M.C.A. building, the Congregational Church, and the People's Church.

The Congregational structure was ornate in design, with a tower at the southeast entrance. The exterior was largely of Marquette red stone, veneered around a heavy timbered interior. In addition to the church auditorium proper, the edifice contained a prayer meeting room, large parlors, kitchen, and pastor's study. The interior arrangement was such that many drafts and cross currents were formed, adding to the difficulty in fighting the fire.

The building, as it stands today, represents an outlay of about $175,000. It was built at a time when material and building wages were at the lowest price. Since the original construction, many minor changes have been made in the edifice, though in the main it stands today as originally planned.

The present pastor is the Rev. Torrance T. Phelps. The congregation numbers about 1,000 and includes in its enrollment many of the most prominent business and professional men of the community.

Before the fire was under control, the trustees of the People's Church formally offered use of their building to the Congregational Church as a temporary home.

The fire was discovered at 6:00 p.m. and, although the church is within a block of Central Fire Station, the entire roof was enveloped in flames before the firemen could reach the scene. Every available piece of fire apparatus in the city was thrown into action. All firemen off duty were called into service, and Kalamazoo's entire force of firemen were swiftly brought into service.

The fire is believed to have started in the south end of the basement of the church and was due either to an overheated furnace or to defective wiring, in the opinion of Fire Chief Charles H. Russell.

BATTLE FLAMES AN HOURFor nearly an hour, the firemen battled with the flames from all sides, the blaze being too fierce for the men to enter the building. Thousands of tons of water were thrown into the flames, but the fire had gained such headway during the first few minutes that it was soon out of control. Frame buildings to the south and east of the church were saved from destruction by being drenched with a continuous downpour of water.

The fire was virtually under control when the tragedy which snuffed out the lives of two of Kalamazoo's most popular firemen occurred.

They had been sent in as a relief squad to firemen who had battled with the blaze from the start. Grasping a line of hose, Slater and Morren, in company with Firemen Ben Kruizenga, Martin Leyden, and others, entered the main entrance to the church to play water on the burning ruins inside.

They had reached the second doors, which open directly into the church proper, and had passed through the west doorway to a distance of about five feet.

As the three men were holding the line of hose, others, who had moved about six feet to one side, heard a crash above and noticed the roof of the second balcony—about 40 feet in the air—give way. They called to their companions to "run for your lives," and dashed to the door. Leyden, thought to have been the nearest to the door, had just crossed the threshold as the falling brick and timbers crashed to the floor. Kruizenga and one or two other firemen also reached safety, but not before falling brick had struck their feet.

SATURDAY, MARCH 14, 1926

THREE LINES OF HOSE BURSTThree different lines of hose burst during the fire, which further handicapped the firemen. These breakages were caused, Chief Russell stated, from excess pressure of the pumps.

Street car traffic was at a standstill during the fire, as power on the trolley wires had to be cut to make it possible for firemen to direct streams of water on the flames from the Rose Street side.

As night fell and the temperature lowered, the firemen became ice-coated and soaked from the spray of water. The streets around the church were flooded to a depth of one to three inches with water.

The walls of the church stood like ghostly spectres with a white coating of ice, and trees near the structure sparkled with frozen spray from the hose lines.

Frank Russell, who conducts a boarding house just east of the church, immediately opened his home to the firemen and prepared coffee and food for them. Other citizens sent sandwiches, and a number of city employees went to Central Station and prepared coffee, which was carried to the firefighters.

Four Kalamazoo Churches Ignited in Three MonthsFirst Methodist Blaze Fifth Serious Visitation of Kind in City During Same Period

Saturday's blaze was the fifth bad fire which has occurred in Kalamazoo within the last three months. It was the fourth church fire in Kalamazoo during the same period.

On December 29, the First Congregational Church was destroyed by a fire which threatened to sweep several nearby structures. The loss was estimated at $175,000. It was partly covered by insurance. Two weeks prior to this blaze, the East Avenue M.E. Church was visited by fire which caused about $5,000 damage.

Closely following the Congregational Church blaze, the Knights of Pythias building on East South Street was swept by fire. The damage was estimated at $10,000. Scores of people in the building at the time the fire broke out were forced to leave by the fire escapes.

On January 26, the heart of Kalamazoo's business district was threatened when the Chase Block at Main and Rose Streets burst into flames shortly after 7:00 a.m. Firemen battled for nearly three hours with the stubborn fire, which virtually gutted the entire building, which was four stories high. The loss ran to more than $100,000.

Last Sunday morning's fire caused $5,000 damage to the Boudeman Building on South Burdick Street. The blaze started on the ground floor and had gained dangerous headway into upper floors when it was discovered. 

SATURDAY, APRIL 10, 1926

METHODIST CHURCH TO BE TORN DOWNPLANS BEING DRAWN FOR NEW EDIFICE AT ACADEMY AND PARK STREETS

Demolition of the ruins of the First Methodist Church structure at Lovell and Rose streets is to be started at once. Trustees of the church have advertised for bids. C.A. Krill, chairman of the board, says no steps will be taken toward the erection of the new church until the return of the Rev. D. Stanley Coors, pastor. He is now in the West, but is expected home early in May.

Ernest Batterson, local architect, is preparing preliminary sketches for the new church, which will be erected at the Academy and Park Street site. Mr. Krill reports minor differences pertaining to fire insurance have been worked out and adjusted, and that a settlement of the insurance will follow in a few days.

WEDNESDAY, JUNE 2, 1926

FIFTH CHURCH FIRE CAUSES $500 LOSSBURNING OF PEWS LAID TO CIGARETTE; SERVICES NOT TO BE INTERRUPTED

For the fifth time in as many months, fire Tuesday night visited a church in Kalamazoo. The blaze was fought for 30 minutes when it was extinguished, leaving a damage of $500.

The blaze started in the main lobby of the First Presbyterian Church, Rose and South streets, less than an hour after Masons had vacated the building after a banquet. A carelessly tossed cigarette stub is believed responsible.

Firemen and apparatus from stations 1, 4, and 6 responded to the alarm at 9:30. Smoke curled through the crevices between the large doors as firemen whined out their hose lines. The blaze was soon under control.

Several pews were burned or scorched, but there was no other damage from the flames, which were confined to the few square yards in which they originated. Most of the damage was from water thrown by the firemen.

The Presbyterian Church was threatened with destruction when a fire started in the motion picture booth several months ago. Tuesday night's church fire brought the number to five since last December when the East Avenue M.E. Church was damaged.

The second also occurred in December when the First Congregational Church was razed. A small fire in the Presbyterian Church was third, and the First Methodist fire last March was fourth.

Services planned for the church building will be carried on without interruption, it was stated Wednesday by officials after an inspection of the building.

WEDNESDAY, JULY 7, 1926

PROBING NEW CHURCH FIRE

CHURCH DESTROYED BY $100,000 FIRE

Fire insurance companies consider churches as select risks and are always anxious to write such business, according to W.H. Pendleton, former president of the Kalamazoo Fire Underwriters' Club.

"Kalamazoo's recent experience with church fires is no criterion for the country over," said Mr. Pendleton. "Churches are good risks and considered profitable and select business. From the insurance viewpoint, they are handled the same as any other property, the risk being considered exclusively."

Mr. Pendleton said that conditions in Kalamazoo had reached a point where skepticism should be cast aside and some effort made to fathom the cause of the many serious church conflagrations here. "I intend to take that matter up at the next meeting of the Kalamazoo Fire Underwriters' Club," said Mr. Pendleton, "and see if some action cannot be taken that will bring results."

No less than seven church fires have occurred in Kalamazoo during the last seven months. Three resulted in total losses, one in heavy damage, and three in slight losses. The list follows:

WEDNESDAY, JULY 7, 1926

PRESBYTERIAN CHURCH FIRE: LOSS $100,000Two Firemen Overcome by Smoke After Collapse of Roof.

DISCOVERED AT 11:20The Presbyterian fire was discovered about 11:20 when the entire interior of the auditorium burst forth in flames. Some of the flames shot out the windows with the first puff.

While apparatus from Central Station a block away was responding to the alarm, patrolmen at the scene broke open the front doors so that firemen might carry hose lines into the structure with the least possible delay. Smoke filled every room in the structure, and the crackling of the flames could be heard from the street. Fire limits were immediately established by the police, and the work of fighting another big church fire got underway within five minutes after the alarm had sounded.

BURSTS INTO FLAMEThe fire, which was the most spectacular of the church conflagrations to watch, is said to have started in or near the pipe organ at the rear of the pulpit. Patrolman Clare Stevens, who turned in the alarm, was the first person to discover the fire. He stated that suddenly the entire interior of the auditorium seemed to burst forth in flames, where a second before all had been darkness. Sergeant John Sperry of the police department saw the first spurt of flames two blocks away.

Upon arrival at the church, Chief Charles H. Russell and several men from Central Station started inside the building through the front entrance. They saw the flames as they shot toward the ceiling in the rear of the auditorium and they sought to fight them at close range. Just as they entered, the main auditorium plaster began falling and the smoke began to thicken. So fierce became the roaring furnace, and great the possibility of injury or death from the falling ceiling, that Chief Russell and his men retreated from the building.

It was while inside the church at the very beginning of the fire that the two firemen, A.C. VanHaaften and Gaare DeRight, were almost overcome by smoke. They were helped from the building by other firemen, and after a rest of a few moments, were unable to resume their posts.

ROOF CRASHESThe front portion of the roof burned rapidly, and just a few moments before it fell into the mass of ruins, the rear portion of the roof burst into flame. The roof of the First Presbyterian Church seemed to burn more rapidly than the roofs of other churches. The firemen were unable to quell the flames on the roof, despite the fact that six different lines of hose were playing water on the top of the building. As the two sections of the church roof fell, a great, resounding puff, not unlike a stifled explosion, was heard, and the flames from the interior of the building shot high into the air, while huge firebrands hurtled from the inside ruins over the traced shell of the walls. Startled by the crash, the crowds started a sudden rush away from the building, fearing that the walls might fall.

After the roof had fallen, the firemen gradually subdued the flames on the inside. Seeing it would be impossible to save the structure itself, Chief Russell concentrated all his efforts on saving the adjoining buildings from igniting. A line of hose was strung to the top floor of the Pythian Building, located just ten feet east of the Presbyterian Church, while another line was carried to the roof of the Y.W.C.A. Lines of water, manned from the ground, were used intermittently to wet the sides of the adjoining buildings which faced the fire.

The walls of the Y.W.C.A., north of the church in South Rose Street, became so heated that a person could not bear his hand against them. The windows in the west side of the Pythian Building were either broken or cracked by the heat. Eight of ten men who were in the Pythian Building, under the leadership of Ralph Chapman, climbed to the fire escape on the west side of the building and wet down the east roof of the church building with fire extinguishers and hose lines from the interior of the Pythian block. When the heat became too intense, the men returned inside the building and poured chemicals from the windows.

Fear was freely expressed that the Pythian Building, which has been on fire two different times within the last eight months, might be ignited by the flames from the church fire, and every precaution was taken to warn everyone in the building of the danger. Officers made a tour of every floor of the building as soon as the church fire started, and people who have offices in the building were notified that they might take from their offices as much of value as they could, pending a possible outbreak in the Pythian building.

Guests, officials, and employees at the Y.W.C.A. Building were routed from their sleep by firemen and officers, and all the occupants were ordered downstairs to the main lobby. The Y.W.C.A., however, was in less danger of ignition than the Pythian Building due to the fact that the light breeze carried the flames in the opposite direction.

The lives of Chief Russell and six of his firemen were endangered when a large section of the west portion of the roof loosened and came crashing to the street on the south side of the building. The chief and his men were engaged at the time, and were in a group moving two or three hose lines about 15 feet from the south wall. As the section of the roof started to slide, the crowd of spectators cried out, and the firemen, looking up, saw the oncoming shower of bricks and timbers. They made a wild dash for safety and reached the center of the street just as the debris struck the ground. Loosened slates from the roof and bricks and heavy pieces of charred wood were raining down continuously during the fire, endangering the firemen and making it difficult to move the hose lines close to the building.

After the fire had abated to some extent, the front entrance of the church was opened, and two lines of hose forced water into the southwest spire, saving it from being gutted. The same move was made in the northwest spire, and that section was also saved from complete ruin.

The entire downtown section of the city and residence areas for many blocks away were clouded with smoke from the fire, and during the time the flames were at their height, the reflection could be seen many miles away from the city. Although there was not a heavy breeze Tuesday night, firebrands were thrown high into the air and were carried as far as a block away.

The fire started at 11:20, and at 2:45 the smoke and fire had cleared away to expose jagged walls of smoke-blackened brick, with charred timbers, twisted iron rods, and debris leaning in a haphazard manner. Inside was a mass of twisted, smoking ruins which about three hours before had been a part of the city's largest church building. The interior of the auditorium was a complete loss, and virtually every other portion of the building was damaged beyond remodeling. The southeastern section fared the best during the fire, and although the roof was burned and fell inside, the interior did not burst forth into flames.

Seeing it would be impossible to save the interior of the church, following the first attempt of firemen to enter the building, Chief Russell issued strict orders that no one was to enter the burning building. When a detail of firemen started with a hose line toward the front entrance, Chief Russell ordered them away. He would not permit his men to go through the doors of the gutted structure until dawn, and he himself surveyed the smoldering ruins from the top floor and roof of the Pythian Block, just 10 feet away. The chief took every precaution to protect the lives of his men, and for three hours after the flames had died down, the firemen remained on the outside to play the streams of water.

RECOVERED IN TWO HOURSIt was nearly two hours before the bodies of the dead firemen were finally dug from the ruins. The rescuers worked in shifts.

Two Kalamazoo firemen were killed, and five others were injured Saturday evening in a fire which swept the First Methodist Church at Rose and Lovell Streets to complete ruin. The loss is estimated at $100,000.

The firemen met death when they were caught beneath a falling balcony at the north end of the church. The balcony fell in as the south side of the big brick tower gave way. Tons of brick and heavy beams came down upon the heads of the firemen almost without warning. Those who escaped barely managed to reach the front vestibule door. Others were caught underneath the wreckage and were helpless until rescued by fellow firemen.

As the three men were holding the line of hose, others, who had moved about six feet to one side, heard a crash above and noticed the roof of the second balcony—about 40 feet in the air—give way. They called to their companions to "run for your lives" and dashed to the door. Leyden, thought to have been the nearest to the door, just crossed the threshold as the falling brick and timbers crashed to the floor. Kruizenga and one or two other firemen also reached safety, but not before falling brick had struck their feet.

Slater and Morren did not reach the door. Both men had gotten within a foot of safety when their bodies were carried through the floor to a depth of two feet. They both fell within a few inches of each other and were undoubtedly crushed to death.

FORMS RESCUE SQUADThe first inkling firemen on the outside of the building had of disaster was when Leyden and others rushed out, shouting that their two companions had been buried beneath the falling roof. Chief Russell immediately organized a rescue party, and a dozen firemen and policemen, armed with axes, shovels, and lanterns, invaded the building, imperiling their own lives as portions of the roof and sections of the brick walls were still sliding to the main floor. No sign of the two firemen could be found at first.

Firemen, policemen, and officers from the sheriff's department began digging, throwing out the timbers and debris as fast as they could. They were severely handicapped for a moment by the high-powered streams of water that were still being played on the ruins and also by darkness. A big searchlight was wired up by Sheriff Borden and suspended over the workmen.

FIND FIRST BODYThe rescuers dug to a depth of one foot before any trace of either fireman was found. Then one shovel struck the leather-coated body of Slater. "I've found one body," informed a fireman. Then they started work with their hands, heaving away the bricks and other debris until half the buried fireman's body was uncovered. Slater was lying face downward. The firemen thought it would be but a few moments before the body would be unearthed, but it required exactly one hour and ten minutes to extricate the body of the first victim.

A huge timber had lodged across Slater's limbs, and firemen had to cut this away with axes. Care had to be used to avoid striking the body, and the firemen who were doing the work were in constant danger of plunging through the floor to the basement. It was necessary for three firemen to hold the body of Slater to keep it from sinking head-downward into the basement.

FELL FACE DOWNWARDFinally, the body was freed and was carried to the H.S. Harrington morgue in the police ambulance. At that time, the body of Morren had not been found. Digging to the eastward of the place where Slater's body had been found, firemen located Morren's body, buried deep in the debris. He, too, had fallen face downward, having been struck just as he was about to pass through the doorway. Less difficulty was experienced in extricating Morren's body, and this gruesome task was completed in half an hour. His body was taken to the Harrington morgue.

CHIEF RUSSELL WEEPSTears coursed down the fire-blackened cheeks of Chief Russell, who had battled the flames for more than an hour and who had warned all his men to be careful in entering the building when the bodies of Slater and Morren were extricated from the ruins.

"Brave men and good firemen," was his exclamation in a broken voice. "We read of these things happening in other cities every day, but when a tragedy of this kind strikes home, it is a different matter." He stated that the men who entered the shell of the building took their lives in their hands, but added further that he had not ordered them so far into the building. If they had fought the burning ruins from the doorway of the first lobby, they would not have been killed, he stated.

The bodies of the firemen were lying across the hose which they had been operating.

MEMBER OF FIREMEN'S BANDSlater had been in the fire department about a year, the chief stated, and was the driver of the hook and ladder truck at Central Station. He was also prominent and popular in the firemen's band and was well-liked by his companions. He leaves a wife and three children.

Morren was stationed at Number 5 Station in Douglas Avenue and had been in the department two years. He was married last June. He leaves two children by a former wife, who died.

James Raven was injured when he entered the basement at the rear to locate the blaze. It is believed he fell, severely injuring one knee. He was carried from the burning building by companions and taken to Old Borgess Hospital in the police car.

WEDNESDAY. MARCH 14, 1926

"BRAVE MEN AND GOOD FIREMEN."

To describe yesterday evening's tragedy as a profound shock to Kalamazoo is to express all too mildly the reaction which the people of this city feel toward the loss of two of their bravest and manliest fellow citizens. We do not wish to indulge in tritely phrased eulogies nor sing a requiem of flowery words, yet we do feel that the highest tribute is not too much to pay to men who have themselves paid their lives as the price of devotion to duty. As Chief Russell put it, we read of these things happening every day in other cities, but when a tragedy of this kind strikes home it is a different matter. And so it is.

The fire department annals of many American communities contain accounts of courageous deeds and heroic sacrifices made by the men who are "always ready," but it takes something closer to home to make us fully appreciate the true significance of the fireman's code of honor.

The destruction of the First Methodist Church edifice, a famous landmark and venerable house of worship though it was, is all but obscured by the inestimably greater loss which its burning entailed. We are certain that the officers and members of the church will be among the first to recognize this. For there is something in the story of John Morren and Claus Slater which gives us new assurance that the world still has men who do not hesitate to hazard their lives in pursuance of their duties, that loyalty to the highest human principles may be sealed as bravely in peace as in war, and that the so-called "softening" influences of modern civilization have not deprived society of men who can face danger as men should.

Heroes? We could go on forever if we wished to argue the question of what constitutes a hero. But we don't think it is necessary to quibble over that. When men are literally struck down in the line of duty, there is no reason whatever to be the least bit niggardly with the praise we ascribe to them.

"Brave men and good firemen" was Chief Russell's brief but thoroughly sufficient comment; we will let it go at that and call it the finest tribute which any commander can pay to those who have served under him. Surely John Morren and Claus Slater would regard it as such. So would the five others who faced death with them in the treacherous recesses of the burning structure.

Kalamazoo is justly proud of John Morren and Claus Slater, but we are certain that even this great feeling of pride is far overshadowed by deep and genuine sympathy for the wives and children who bear the real brunt of yesterday's tragic happening. No words of comfort or consolation can soften more than slightly the anguish of their trial; no eulogies of the dead can help them bear a loss so bitter. We only hope that their spirits may be strengthened by the knowledge that the men they mourn died as men. And it is not to be thought, for one moment, that the people of Kalamazoo will allow the bereaved ones to endure any kind of suffering which it is within the people's power to prevent.

FRIDAY, MARCH 19, 1926

THE BEST REWARD FOR VALOR.

Whether or not the children of Claus Slater and John Morren will be given an education like other boys and girls is a question which the people of Kalamazoo must answer—and answer soon. The appeal made in their behalf by the local underwriters' club ought to receive a most generous popular response. We doubt if there were ten people in this entire city last Sunday morning who did not express, either audibly or to themselves, a noble desire to "do something" for the families of the two firemen whose unselfish devotion to duty cost them their lives in the First Methodist Church fire. The opportunity is now open to everyone who feels that such courageous conduct ought to be rewarded in the best possible way.

We think it is best to speak with perfect frankness on this matter. The fund of about $600 subscribed up to the present is altogether too small an amount to represent two days of campaigning in this worthy cause. The news stories published in The Gazette have stated plainly the conditions of the Slater and Morren families, and the public should have no difficulty in determining just how much is needed to make this movement a success. The three Slater children are all less than five years of age. The oldest one is supposed to start school next fall. The two Morren children are already in school, but they have many years to go before even their elementary education is complete. Unless the people of Kalamazoo give better monetary expression to their feelings of sympathy than has been evident thus far, the school careers of these five youngsters may be seriously endangered.

We trust that no one will be misled by the announcement, published yesterday, that "there is no immediate need of relief" for the Slater and Morren families. That simply means that the bereaved wives and children of the two firemen are not in perilous financial circumstances at the present moment. Their case, in other words, is not one for charity. And we may as well emphasize right now that the element of "charity" does not enter into the problem at all. The citizens who are sponsoring this movement are merely asking the people of Kalamazoo to make good on their obligation to Claus Slater and John Morren; to give assurance that the children of these heroic firemen will be given as good schooling as would have been theirs had their fathers not been forced to die in the service of the community.

The tragedy of last Saturday night was one of the worst shocks which has come to Kalamazoo in a long time. A wave of sincere, genuine human sympathy literally swept the entire city. Now there is a splendid chance to translate that sympathy into something tangible, something truly expressive of the way Kalamazoo feels toward men in its public service who do not hesitate to make the supreme sacrifice when duty calls. The people cannot hope to compensate these children for the loss of their brave fathers. That is not the point. But the people can pay at least a part of their debt to Claus Slater and John Morren; they can, if they will, build up a fund to assure these children of the chance in life which men of such character as their fathers would have given them. That is fair enough, isn't it?

FUTURE OF SIX FATHERLESS TOTS, WHOSE DADDIES GAVE LIVES TO CITY, DEPENDS ON RELIEF DRIVE.

How Will Kalamazoo Reward Firemen’s Supreme Devotion to Duty; Question Asked in Campaign.

In two modest Kalamazoo homes, one in Douglas Avenue and the other in Lumm Avenue, a pall of sorrow has enveloped two bereaved mothers and six fatherless little tots—made fatherless by their daddies, John Morren and Claus Slater, who gave their lives fighting the First Methodist Church fire a week ago.

Hearts of a sympathizing community have gone out to these grief-stricken families, and the people of Kalamazoo and many surrounding cities have paid tribute to the memory of the two brave firemen who were crushed beneath falling roofs and walls. Funeral rites have been conducted for the firemen, and the sorrowing families have returned to homes shrouded in sadness and fatherless forever.

WILL KALAMAZOO AID?But will Kalamazoo sit by and permit the widowed mothers to carry on alone in the care and protection of their six fatherless children?

This is the question the Underwriters’ Club of Kalamazoo is asking residents of the community. It is a question which the club believes can be answered best by means of contributions to a fund which is now being raised for the care and education of the little children who are destined to pass through their most tender years without the kindly aid and guidance of a father. 

SUNDAY, MARCH 21, 1926

HOPES TO RAISE $4,000.Touched by the tragedy of a week ago, the Underwriters’ club took the first step to promote a relief fund for the bereaved families. By means of voluntary subscriptions, the club hopes to obtain a fund of $6,000 at least, “A thousand dollars a child,” is the slogan adopted by the club.

More than $1,000 had been contributed to the fund by Saturday night, it was announced by the committee promoting the campaign. This represents contributions received during four days. It represents the amount the club would like to put in trust for one child, and there are six little tots whose future welfare may now rest in the hands of the community.

Burdened by grief and weighted down by anxiety over obligations, the brave little mothers are looking somewhat dubiously into the dim future, wondering what may be their fortune—good or bad? Their only concern now is whether or not they can provide for their little ones, three of whom are too young to realize the tragedy which has befallen them. The mothers, neither of whom are in a position to take employment, are focused on the care of the children.

MISSES HER DADDY.The Morren children are Evelyn, nine years; Frances, seven years; and Dorothy, three years. Evelyn and Frances are old enough to realize the death of their father, while Dorothy is too young to understand. She plays innocently, while her mother and sisters grieve.

Donna Marie Slater, four years old, and Richard Leo Slater, three years old, know that their father is missing, but they do not fully understand the tragedy. These children, like the others, are too young to comprehend the full loss.

Two of the Morren children, Evelyn and Frances, go to school. Donna Marie and Richard Leo will start school within a year or two. The mothers are determined that each child will receive an education.

IN DEBT ON HOMES.The financial benefits the bereaved families will receive, including the Workingmen’s Compensation, will only cover immediate needs. Benefits will not be paid in a lump sum but spread over 300 weeks, with a weekly allowance of $14 per family. In one family, there is a small insurance policy, but all these benefits will not be sufficient to cover the indebtedness on the two homes, which amounts to $6,000.

Firemen from Kalamazoo, the Metropolitan Club, and other agencies have offered assistance, but the current campaign is aimed at raising a trust fund for the children's future needs, including their education.  The movement will not end until the quota of $6,000 is reached.

THURSDAY, JULY 8, 1926

ANOTHER CHURCH FIREIn the complete destruction by fire of the First Presbyterian Church edifice at Rose and South Streets, Kalamazoo loses another one of its most familiar landmarks. The large building destroyed Tuesday night was erected 42 years ago to replace the structure which had met a similar fate in 1883. While the passing of this venerable house of worship cannot be attended by a rather general feeling of regret throughout the city, there is some consolation in the knowledge that it probably would not have remained standing much longer anyway. The first unit of the new home of the First Presbyterian Church Society, on South Street, one block west of the old site, is rapidly nearing completion, and when finished it will be one of the most attractive works of architecture in Kalamazoo. It is unfortunate that the members of the Presbyterian congregation will be forced to undergo the inconvenience of holding their services in emergency quarters until their new edifice is ready for occupancy.

The First Presbyterian Church is the third large church building in Kalamazoo to have been demolished by flames within the last few months. The people of this city will long remember the First Methodist Episcopal Church disaster, in which two of our courageous fire fighters lost their lives. Sometime before that, the Congregational Church was burned beyond repair. Fire started twice in the Presbyterian building prior to Tuesday night but was successfully combated before any serious damage had been done. Lodge halls also appear to have been favorite victims of the epidemic of destructive blazes which have been sweeping this city. The rooms of the Knights of Columbus were utterly ruined by the conflagration which all but destroyed the Chase block; the Moose Temple on Portage Street suffered a heavy loss from the flames, and fires of less serious proportions have invaded the Knights of Pythias building twice and the Odd Fellows Hall on Main Street.

Of course, it is impossible to point to any definite evidence to the effect that a firebug has been operating in Kalamazoo. But it is equally impossible to show any other substantial cause for the repeated attacks by fire on lodge halls and church buildings. Because of their structure and the fact that they are unoccupied the greater part of the time, churches make easy "material" for the demented incendiary. If he can gain entrance, he can go about his destructive business unobserved, and flames, once started in such a building, make rapid headway. The lodge halls which have burned in Kalamazoo recently have all been situated in closely built downtown sections, and if the fires had not been checked as efficiently as they were, the resulting spectacles would have been such as to delight the eyes of a confirmed pyromaniac.

The three investigations which have been started by local and state authorities are quite in order.

Second Congregational church, erected in 1852, and (below), the Rev. W. A. Waterman, pastor at the time the structure recently burned was built.

Opinion is divided regarding the origin of the fire. WEDNESDAY, MARCH 24, 1926

RED HOT FLUE RAZED CHURCH, PROBERS FINDOverheated Furnace Blamed for Destruction of Methodist Edifice.

That the First Methodist church fire March 13 started from an overheated furnace and flue, was the report of an official investigating committee composed of Fire Chief Charles H. Russell, Sheriff Jerome S. Borden, Acting Police Chief Roy W. Carney, and Frank Pfaltz, contractor, Tuesday.

A thorough investigation of the ruins at the church site was conducted by this committee in a determined effort to learn the origin of the fire which claimed the lives of two firemen and wrought property damage estimated above $100,000.

MAKE THOROUGH PROBEEach of the four furnaces in the church basement, all of the flue lines, and all electrical wiring that could be traced, were examined by the committee and at the conclusion of the inspection there remained but one plausible theory as to the origin of the fire. Evidence found in and around the furnace which stood in the basement directly beneath the church organ, led the committee to believe the heating unit had become dangerously hot, setting fire to the floor joists.

The flue to the furnace in question is at present a rosy red color, indicating that it had been red hot. Other flues are still shiny and show no evidence of having been overheated. The joists and floor above the furnace are charcoal, while the joists in other parts of the first floor are only smoke and soot-stained.

SET FIRE TO JOISTS.The investigating committee is certain the fire started in the floor joists above the furnace near the rear of the basement, eating through the floor and coursing up a partition to the roof. Wooden shingles, said to have been beneath the newer roof covering, furnished fuel to the rapidly spreading flames.

Sheriff Borden, who announced the committee's report, stated that all four of the furnaces had been well filled with coal and had evidently been "banked" with coal. Electrical wiring units in the building, as many as could be inspected, were found in such a condition as not to produce fire.

WEDNESDAY, JULY 7, 1926.

Flames breaking out at 11:20 pm completely destroy structure at Rose and South Streets.

SET BY FIREBUG, BELIEF

Officials Promise Drastic Action to Apprehend Incendiary After Long Series of Fires.

Believing that the 12 church and lodge hall fires in Kalamazoo since December, 1925, may have been in part, at least, of incendiary origin, a city and state investigation which may lead to the apprehension of a firebug, was begun early Wednesday morning by Police Chief Roy W. Carney, Deputy State Fire Marshal Frank Burmaster, and Sheriff Jerome S. Borden.

Deputy Warden Burmaster announced shortly before noon that two officials from the state fire marshal’s office at Lansing would arrive in Kalamazoo during the day to join with the local officers in investigating, not only the First Presbyterian church fire Tuesday night, but also the other major church and lodge hall blazes.

BELIEVE FIREBUG DID IT

The police and sheriff’s departments and the deputy fire marshal will conduct separate investigations, but will co-operate in all the work.

Opinion that the series of disastrous fires—at least those involving churches and lodge halls—were incendiary were emphatically expressed following the Presbyterian church fire and the officials themselves are strongly of the opinion that a firebug is at work in Kalamazoo.

In each of the previous big fires the police, sheriff, and state fire marshal officials conducted investigations but found no evidence which would point conclusively to incendiarism.

INDICATE ARSON, BELIEF

“The fact there have been so many big fires, under conditions which indicate incendiarism at regular intervals during the past several months, indicates that at least some of these blazes have been set,” declared Chief of Police Carney.

“If a firebug is at work, it may be difficult to apprehend him, unless he should be discovered in the act of setting another fire. Virtually all the evidence of incendiarism is destroyed with the burning of the buildings.”

Sheriff Borden and Deputy Marshal Burmaster agreed with the chief that the series of fires would arouse suspicion that a firebug was at work in Kalamazoo. “We will leave no stone unturned in our investigation of the fire,” declared Sheriff Borden.

TOTAL LOSS $700,000

With the destruction of the First Presbyterian church and the lodge halls, Kalamazoo’s total fire loss for the past six months reached approximately $700,000.

All of this property, including churches and other valuable buildings within the past six months, has been destroyed and in this period there have been 15 fires, three of which have destroyed buildings as many as three different times.

Officers called attention to the fact that every one of the major fires has occurred either between 5:00 in the afternoon and 12:00 midnight, at noontime, early in the morning before working hours, or during the forenoon of holidays.

Two of the disastrous fires—the First Methodist church and the Trades and Labor Council hall, occurred about 5:00 Saturday afternoon.

Three of the fires—the East Avenue M. E. church, the Herfendahl store and the Boudenman block, occurred Sunday forenoons.

WEDNESDAY, JULY 7, 1926.

PRESBYTERIAN CHURCH FIRE LOSS $100,000Third Downtown Religious Structure to Be Razed; Aggregate Damage $700,000.

BARE WALLS STANDINGTwo Firemen Overcome by Smoke After Collapse of Roof.

Fire, believed to have been of incendiary origin, destroyed the First Presbyterian church building at South and Rose streets at 11:30 Tuesday night, with a loss estimated at $100,000 to $125,000.

Two firemen were partially overcome by smoke and gas fumes and seven others narrowly escaped injury or death when a portion of the roof fell to the street. The fire endangered a score or more downtown blocks.

TWO PREVIOUS FIRES

It was the third fire which had broken out in that structure within the last four months. The first fire, late in the winter, had started near a moving picture booth. The second blaze, which occurred during the Knights Templar conclave here in June, was discovered at 11:00 in the evening near the front end of the building. That fire did considerable damage to the front lobby, but was extinguished before it spread.

The Presbyterian church fire Tuesday night was the third big church blaze to occur in Kalamazoo this year. The First Congregational church, just one block from the ill-fated Presbyterian building, was the first large church to be completely destroyed. The First Methodist church fire, March 13, was the second major blaze and the one which claimed the lives of two firemen.

In addition to these three major church fires in which the destruction has been virtually complete, there have been smaller blazes discovered in places of worship—two of which were extinguished in East Avenue M. E. church, badly gutted in December, 1925, while the Church of Christ was visited by a small fire.

15 FIRES SINCE DEC. 16

The Presbyterian fire constituted the fifteenth big fire in Kalamazoo since Dec. 16, 1925. Of these 15 fires, 12 of them were in churches or in buildings in which fraternal organizations held meetings.

The complete list of the major fires, which resulted in an aggregate loss of approximately $700,000, follows:

First Congregational church (complete loss), $175,000.

BUILDINGS ENDANGERED.

Among the buildings which were endangered by the Presbyterian church fire were:

The Pythian building, 10 feet east of the church.

Y.W.C.A. building, 30 feet north of the church.

Public Library, directly across the street.

Park club, on the southwest corner of South and Rose streets.

Odd Fellows temple, directly west of the Park club.

City hall, just west of the Odd Fellows' temple.

Post office, at Burdick and South streets.

Boudenman block, just east of the Pythian building.

A close watch was kept at all these buildings to warn the firemen if any portion of the roofs should be ignited by firebrands. Police and sheriff's officers established "beats" to and from these buildings. None of the surrounding structures were ignited, however.

Danger warnings were posted on the church building Wednesday morning by Building Inspector Alexander C. Leek, following an inspection of the ruins. The walls which are left standing are considered dangerous as are also the ruins which are piled high on the inside of the structure.

The fire, occurring at the time it did, held up street car traffic all over the city. Cars bound for their last run of the night were brought to a standstill when the power on all the lines was shut off and the long lines of hose were drawn across the tracks.

Every fireman and every piece of fire-fighting equipment was pressed into service with the outbreak of the fire. All reserve firemen were called on duty and remained on duty throughout the night. So dangerous did the fire become that the reserve truck, which is always kept at Central Station for emergency calls during a big fire, was rushed to the scene and kept in action.

Pumpers were located on every corner surrounding the church block and these powerful machines shot streams of water into the various hose lines at the rate of from 1,400 to 1,500 gallons per minute. Ten hose lines, eight shooting water directly into the building and two being manned from the Y.W.C.A. and Pythian building, were kept in operation continuously during the fire.

With the sounding of the general alarm, the Salvation Army, the stewards at the Odd Fellows' temple, and Mrs. Jerome S. Borden, matron at the County jail, prepared coffee, doughnuts, and sandwiches for the firemen.

WEDNESDAY, JULY 9, 1926.

PRESBYTERIANS WILL MEET AT H.S. AUDITORIUMOfficials Continue Probe of Fire Which Destroyed Edifice.

First Presbyterian church services for the next year, at least, will be held in the Central high school auditorium, it was announced Friday, following a conference of church trustees with the board of education.

The Sunday services, July 11, will be held at 11:00 in the forenoon, in the auditorium. The union services in the evening will be conducted at the First Baptist church.

MEET WITH BOARD.

A committee composed of Dr. J. T. Upjohn and George Irwin conferred with the board of education on behalf of the church trustees and made arrangements for use of the church. The Presbyterian board believes it will be fully a year before a new church will be near completion.

Sunday school at the Presbyterian church has been discontinued for the summer and there are no meetings now of the young people's society.

The board of trustees met Thursday afternoon with officials of a firm of architects in Chicago for the purpose of going over the plans for the new church building and completing arrangements for starting the work on the new structure. The plans soon will be completed, it was announced.

PROBE CONTINUES.

No new developments in the two investigations being conducted into the Presbyterian church fire were reported Friday by the sheriff's and police departments nor by Deputy State Fire Marshal Frank Burmaster, who is working with two men from the state fire marshal’s office.

The investigation is to continue, however, and will cover not only the Presbyterian church fire but also the several other church and lodge hall fires which have occurred in Kalamazoo during the last six months.

WEDNESDAY, DECEMBER 30, 1925.

BURNED CHURCH TO BE REBUILT BY FALL OF '26Preliminary Plans Call for Starting of Construction in Spring.

WALLS TO BE TORN DOWNServices to Continue in People's Church; Flames Stop Plans for Enlarging Old Edifice.

On the site where this morning drab, skeleton-like walls stood as sentinels guarding the ruins of the First Congregational church, destroyed by Tuesday afternoon's $175,000 fire, a completely new and modern structure will rise. The congregation, undaunted by the colossal loss, will continue regular services in the People's church until the fall of 1926, when, it is expected, the new edifice will be ready for occupancy.

While weary and frozen firemen fought to save nearby buildings and to extinguish the smoldering wreckage of what had been Kalamazoo's largest Protestant church Tuesday afternoon, a joint meeting of the First Congregational church boards accepted the balance of fate with a spirit of optimism. The board of trustees and the advisory board members convened together and accepted an invitation to continue, without a break, regular services in the People's church. Immediate steps to build a new structure, complete and modern in every way, were taken.

PLANNING NEW CHURCH.

The joint board meeting members decided to appoint a building committee to formulate plans for a new First Congregational church and arrange to submit preliminary sketches for adoption by the congregation at an early date. Robert F. Staebler was named chairman of the committee consisting of L. W. Sutherland, Frank D. Fuller, Guy Wilson, W. O. Jones, A. B. MacDonald, and Donald S. Gillmore, who will work with the Rev. Torrance T. Phelps, pastor, in drawing up preliminary plans.

"Our church rises with optimism and energy to meet the urgent challenge," the Rev. Phelps, pastor, declares. "The walls of the old building, built in 1890, will be torn down and, if initial plans are carried out, work will start in early spring so that the new and more modern house of worship will be available by next fall."

FRIDAY, APRIL 2, 1926.

START WORK ON $250,000 CHURCH BY MAY 15, PLANAction of Congregational Board Unanimous, Announcement.GOING UP ON OLD SITEWill Contain Stage, Church House, Nave to Seat 800 Persons, and Walled Garden.

The erection of one of the finest church structures in Western Michigan was assured Thursday afternoon when the trustees of the First Congregational church of this city voted unanimously to enter into contract with Amar Embury II, New York architect, for the plans and specifications for such a building. According to Robert E. Staebler, chairman of the building committee, Mr. Embury is to have complete specifications ready within three weeks. Following their receipt, bids will be asked. It is hoped to begin actual construction work not later than May 15.

The trustees met at 4:00 Thursday afternoon in the People’s church parlors. Martin H. DeLoof, chairman of the board, presided. The building committee had been in session with Embury on Monday. At that time they considered several plans. Their final selection met with favor on the part of the trustees. While no estimates have been attempted as to the costs of the new church home, it is the general opinion that the structure will represent an outlay of from $225,000 to $250,000. This amount does not include site, organ, furnishings, or equipment.

PLANS WALLED GARDEN.

The new First Congregational church will be erected at Academy and South Park streets, on the site occupied for the last 75 years by churches of this denomination. It will replace the structure totally destroyed by fire. The plan accepted calls for a building Renaissance gothic in type and provides not only for a church proper, but in addition a well-arranged church house, its general layout, the church proper facing Academy street and extends north and south, while the church house connects in the rear and extends east and west the width of the property. The site is not only sufficient to accommodate these two structures, but will at the same time permit of a walled garden, 64x88 feet in dimensions, being located on the plat immediately at Academy and Park streets. This attractive green and floral setting will add materially to the general effectiveness of the whole.
